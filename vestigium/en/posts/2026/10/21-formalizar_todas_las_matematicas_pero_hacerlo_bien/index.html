    Formalize all of mathematics, but do it right

    José A. Alonso

    21-10-2026

    Also available in:

    Español

    Review of "How to Formalize All of Mathematics", by Jeremy Avigad, Kevin Buzzard, Johan Commelin, Rémy Degenne, Kim Morrison, Oliver Nash and Adam Topaz, published on October 16, 2026.

    AI has become extraordinarily good at proving theorems, both formally and informally. Some enthusiasts have concluded that the time has come to formalize all of mathematics. Seven prominent figures from the Lean and Mathlib community respond with a text that supports the idea but urges slowing down.

    A "Yes" with conditions

    The authors are optimistic about AI, but they fear the race to formalize everything as quickly as possible. Done well, the effort would enormously expand mathematical discovery. Done badly, it would be a waste of resources and, most likely, would cause irreparable damage. A poor formalization of existing results, they warn, is worse than none at all.

    Formalization as a tool

    A formal artifact that nobody uses is worth little. We formalize in order to do better mathematics: to explore generalizations, connect different branches, and refine concepts. That is why they prefer to discard deficient AI-generated code and redo it properly, with plenty of help from AI itself, rather than clean it up.

    The map of projects

    • Mathlib: the community's open-source library, with steady growth and human curation.
    • Formal Conjectures: formal statements of open problems.
    • The Annals Challenge: stating, without proving, the theorems of the Annals of Mathematics.
    • Tau Ceti: a library written by AI and adversarially reviewed by other AIs.
    • Formal Frontier: AI agents that turn texts into formal libraries. It already has 41 published.
    • Palomar: a registry of formal projects aimed at specific results.

    Ten values for formalizing well

    Derived from the Mathlib community's values document, they apply to any formal library:

    1. Rely on human-managed libraries, in particular Mathlib.
    2. Prefer thoroughness and breadth.
    3. Avoid duplication and generalize theorems.
    4. Review additions publicly.
    5. Publish freely, with open-source and interoperable code.
    6. Stay compatible with every version of Lean and Mathlib.
    7. Keep an open mind to change.
    8. Document and organize.
    9. Link to the existing literature.
    10. Set standards and stick to them: leaving bad code to "fix it later" is counterproductive.

    What they propose

    The authors estimate that within three years, perhaps sooner, most of mathematics could be formalized. To achieve this, they call for contributing to Mathlib, providing resources to Tau Ceti and Formal Frontier, drafting roadmaps, and creating a non-profit platform, with mathematicians in control of the tools.

    Assessment

    The most valuable thing about the text is that it frames quality as a question of governance: who reviews, by what standards, and what the output will be used for. Its list of values deserves to be read in full, even by those who don't use Lean.
